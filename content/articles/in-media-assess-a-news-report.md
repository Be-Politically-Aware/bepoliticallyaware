A useful news report should make it possible to understand what happened, what evidence supports that account and what is still uncertain. Start with the report itself rather than deciding solely from whether you like the outlet.

## Follow the sourcing

Does the report identify the document, recording, dataset or witness behind its important claim? Open the linked material. Check whether it supports the headline as well as the body.

Distinguish independent corroboration from several articles repeating the same press release. An official response matters, but it does not automatically settle the underlying dispute.

## Read the labels and the missing context

Is the item reporting, opinion, advertising, satire or a transcript of someone's claim? “A minister said X” and “X happened” are different propositions.

Check whether an allegation is described as an allegation and whether a relevant response or later order appears. An accurate sentence can still leave a misleading impression if the report omits context that changes its meaning.

## Examine incentives without inventing a conspiracy

Look for documented ownership, funding, sponsorship or other relevant conflicts. Link the evidence for the conflict and explain why it matters to this coverage.

Do not jump from “this outlet has a conflict” to “every sentence it publishes is false”. Equally, a source is not automatically reliable because it criticises a government or supports a cause you agree with. Consistent scrutiny is more useful than an unsourced list of “sold-out media”.

## Ask how errors and sources are handled

Reuters' published standards, for example, address accuracy, transparent correction of errors and careful source attribution, including conditions for anonymous sourcing. Those are declared standards, not independent proof that every Reuters article meets them. They offer concrete questions to ask of any report. [1](https://reutersagency.com/about/standards-values/)

An unnamed source is not automatically fabricated, but the report should provide enough context to assess why the source is relevant and what is independently established. A named source is not automatically correct either.

## Your verdict can be narrower than “trust”

You might conclude: “The linked court order supports this procedural update, but the article's claim about motive is not established.” That is more useful than accepting or rejecting an entire outlet as a single package.

Keep the report and its evidence separate in your notes. When a correction appears, check whether it changes your own interpretation and anything you previously shared.
