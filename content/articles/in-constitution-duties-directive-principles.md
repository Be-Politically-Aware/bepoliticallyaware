“What about your duties?” does not answer a question about a government decision. “I have rights” does not explain every responsibility a person has either. Before arguing about which matters more, identify which constitutional category is being discussed.

## Three different questions

**Fundamental Rights** concern protected freedoms and other constitutional guarantees in Part III, with constitutional remedies for their enforcement. **Directive Principles** in Part IV guide the State’s policy choices. **Fundamental Duties** in Article 51A are duties of citizens. They should not be merged into a single list of promises that all operate in the same way. [1](https://www.legislative.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/07/c9fe9c9b6840524844316f74bb1c556c.pdf#page=50) [2](https://www.legislative.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/07/c9fe9c9b6840524844316f74bb1c556c.pdf#page=52) [3](https://www.legislative.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/07/c9fe9c9b6840524844316f74bb1c556c.pdf#page=56)

Article 37 says that Directive Principles are not enforceable by a court in themselves, while treating them as fundamental to governance and requiring the State to apply them when making laws. “Not directly enforceable” therefore does not mean “irrelevant” or “permission to ignore them”. [2](https://www.legislative.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/07/c9fe9c9b6840524844316f74bb1c556c.pdf#page=52)

For a policy discussion, ask how a proposal advances a stated constitutional aim, what resources it needs and how its effects will be measured. For a legal claim, identify the actual enforceable provision, law or order involved rather than treating every policy aspiration as an immediately available individual remedy.

## Inquiry is part of the text too

Article 51A includes scientific temper, humanism, inquiry and reform, alongside responsibilities concerning harmony, the environment and public property. A description of duties that mentions only obedience leaves out important parts of the text. [3](https://www.legislative.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/07/c9fe9c9b6840524844316f74bb1c556c.pdf#page=56)

For this site’s purpose, there is a practical connection: asking for the original evidence, noticing an error and changing a conclusion are ways to take inquiry seriously. That is an editorial interpretation of the principle, not a claim that the Constitution prescribes a particular fact-checking website or requires agreement with our articles.

**Hypothetical example:** residents ask why a public clinic lacks staff. Someone replies that residents should keep their neighbourhood clean. Cleanliness may be a worthwhile responsibility, but it does not establish whether the clinic’s sanctioned posts are filled. The staffing question still needs staffing evidence.

Reverse the example as well. A person protesting a poor service should not assume that the importance of the complaint makes every possible method of protest lawful. Evaluate the service failure and the conduct separately.

## Make a policy argument specific

Start with a concrete aim: better access to education, cleaner air, or equal treatment. Identify the proposed action and its trade-offs. Ask which institution has authority, what the applicable law requires and what evidence would show improvement. A reference to constitutional values can clarify the aim, but cannot replace those steps.

Nor should a reader be told to prove perfect conduct before their complaint is considered. Nothing in this page supplies an official with a general licence to withhold an applicable legal protection because they dislike a citizen’s behaviour.

The useful distinction is between a constitutional guarantee, a direction for public policy and a duty of citizenship. Keep all three visible. Do not use one as a distraction from a question about another.
