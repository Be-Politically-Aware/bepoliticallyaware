Political funding can raise a legitimate accountability question before anyone has proved a crime. The task is to state that question accurately and investigate it without turning suspicion into a verdict.

## Separate the records from the inference

Begin with what each document establishes. A payment record concerns money. A tender award concerns a procurement decision. A meeting record concerns contact. None automatically explains the motive behind the others.

**Hypothetical example:** Company A makes a political contribution and later wins a contract. That sequence justifies checking the tender criteria, competing bids, decision-makers and any documented relationship. It does not, by timing alone, establish that the contribution bought the contract.

Ask what an alternative explanation would predict. Was the firm already supplying the same product? Did it satisfy published requirements? Were those requirements changed in a way that needs explanation? Could the apparent link be a mistaken match between companies with similar names? These are investigation questions, not invented findings about a real company.

## What the electoral-bond decisions establish

The Supreme Court’s order of 2 August 2024 recounts the earlier judgment invalidating the electoral-bond scheme. It separately considers petitions seeking investigation of alleged wrongdoing. In paragraphs 15–17, the Court explains why invoking its Article 32 jurisdiction at that stage was premature without first pursuing ordinary legal remedies. [1](https://api.sci.gov.in/supremecourt/2024/17622/17622_2024_1_30_54391_Order_02-Aug-2024.pdf#page=1) [2](https://api.sci.gov.in/supremecourt/2024/17622/17622_2024_1_30_54391_Order_02-Aug-2024.pdf#page=6)

That order should not be rewritten as a finding that every disclosed donation was corrupt. Nor should it be rewritten as a finding that every transaction was innocent. The constitutional challenge and transaction-specific allegations ask different questions.

ECI’s disclosure notice also says that the SBI data was uploaded as received. Hosting a disclosure is not the same as independently proving the truth of every inference later drawn from it. [3](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2015989)

## A conflict question is more specific than “they know each other”

Identify the decision-maker’s role, the relevant interest, the decision and the applicable conflict or procurement rule. Ask whether the interest was disclosed, whether the person participated and what the decision record says. Do not invent a universal prohibition that is not in the applicable rule.

For procurement, the central goods manual describes evaluation in terms of responsiveness, qualification and the lowest evaluated bid, and sets out award-publication requirements with exceptions. Those records can help test an allegation, but the applicable tender and jurisdiction still matter. [4](https://www.iitk.ac.in/centralstores/data/Manual_Goods_2024-17-09-24.pdf#page=201) [5](https://www.iitk.ac.in/centralstores/data/Manual_Goods_2024-17-09-24.pdf#page=203)

A fair account records the subject’s response and any evidence that weakens the allegation. This is not a requirement to treat all explanations as equally convincing. It is a requirement to assess them rather than omit inconvenient information.

## Publish a bounded conclusion

Useful conclusions can be modest: “The records establish a payment and an award, but not a causal link”; “the tender criteria changed, and the reason is not in the retrieved documents”; or “the court made this particular finding on this date”. Explain how additional evidence could change the assessment.

Avoid guilt scores generated from donations, names or photographs. Do not expose private contact details or identity-document numbers merely because a source file contains them. Keep a correction history and place a material later outcome alongside the earlier claim.
