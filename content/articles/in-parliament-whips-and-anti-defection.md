Representatives are elected as individuals, often on a party ticket. What happens when a member disagrees with that party in the legislature? The answer involves both political discipline and the Constitution’s anti-defection provisions.

## What a whip does

In parliamentary usage, “whip” can mean a party official organising its members or an instruction concerning parliamentary business and voting. A party’s instruction is not itself the Speaker’s decision that a member has lost their seat. Keep the instruction, alleged breach and formal decision separate. [1](https://sansad.in/uploads/Chapter5_bcef32a522_57941e8421.pdf?updated_at=2022-09-14T12%3A00%3A45.541Z#page=1)

The Tenth Schedule includes disqualification grounds involving voluntarily giving up party membership and voting or abstaining contrary to an authorised party direction, subject to the stated permission and condonation provisions. [2](https://www.legislative.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/07/c9fe9c9b6840524844316f74bb1c556c.pdf#page=376)

## It is not simply “you can never disagree”

Distinguish a disagreement expressed in discussion from the particular conduct alleged to trigger disqualification. Ask what direction was issued, who was authorised to issue it, which vote it concerned and what the member actually did. Do not infer every legally relevant fact from a news photograph or a comment outside the House.

**Hypothetical example:** a member criticises a proposed spending programme in a speech but votes with the party. Another abstains from a directed vote. These are not identical events. Determining a legal consequence requires the applicable provision and the factual record, not a rule that “criticism equals defection”.

A breach may be politically controversial without every necessary legal question being settled. Conversely, calling an action a matter of conscience does not remove an applicable disqualification rule.

## Be precise about the merger provision

Paragraph 4 contains a merger exception with a two-thirds legislature-party condition. It is not a general permission for any two-thirds group to do anything without consequence. The original-party and merger requirements also matter. The earlier one-third “split” provision was removed; it should not be quoted as the operative exception. [2](https://www.legislative.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/07/c9fe9c9b6840524844316f74bb1c556c.pdf#page=376)

These details are why a viral claim that “two-thirds have left, so the case is automatically over” needs scrutiny. Read the specific decision and reasoning in the actual dispute before claiming the conditions have been met.

## Who decides, and what should a reader record?

Paragraph 6 gives the disqualification question to the Chairman or Speaker of the relevant House. The official compilation also records the invalidation of paragraph 7’s attempted court-jurisdiction bar. Do not describe the process as an ECI decision or assume a presiding officer’s order is immune from judicial scrutiny. [2](https://www.legislative.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/07/c9fe9c9b6840524844316f74bb1c556c.pdf#page=376)

A usable case timeline distinguishes the alleged conduct, petition, notices or hearings, reasoned order and any later court order. A pending petition is not a completed disqualification. This article is not a status report on any named member or party dispute.

## The policy question remains open to debate

When evaluating these rules, separate two concerns: whether a government can maintain reliable support, and whether legislators can scrutinise proposals independently. Ask how a proposed reform would handle each. Stating that tension is not an instruction to ignore the law, nor proof that every use of a whip serves the public interest.
