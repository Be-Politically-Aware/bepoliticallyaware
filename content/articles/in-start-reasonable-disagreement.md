# How can people disagree without abandoning evidence?

Two people can agree on a number and disagree about what should be done. They can also claim to disagree about values while relying on incompatible factual assumptions.

Separating these layers makes an argument easier to examine.

## What happened?

Start with checkable claims. Was money approved or spent? What does the report cover? What did the order actually decide?

For example, the Union Budget separately labels Budget Estimates, Revised Estimates and actuals. Two people using different columns may appear to disagree about a single amount when they are answering different questions. [1](https://www.indiabudget.gov.in/doc/Budget_at_Glance/bag1.pdf#page=3)

Resolve the definition and period before interpreting the difference.

## What might happen next?

A prediction needs assumptions. Ask what must be true for the proposed result to follow, what could change the outcome and how the prediction will be evaluated.

Do not present a forecast as an observed fact. Nor should the existence of uncertainty make every forecast equally plausible: compare the supporting evidence and reasoning.

## What should matter most?

In a **hypothetical bus-policy debate**, one person might prioritise lower fares while another prioritises increasing frequency within a constrained budget. Evidence about cost, usage and available capacity can inform the argument. A remaining disagreement may concern the weight each person gives to those goals.

Name that choice. “We disagree about which benefit should take priority” is clearer than accusing the other person of rejecting all evidence.

## Fairness is not false balance

Represent the opposing argument accurately, but do not give an unsupported claim equal standing simply to make the presentation symmetrical. A well-established fact and an unevidenced allegation need different treatment.

The same standard applies when one side is your own. A documented correction is not something to conceal because it weakens a preferred argument.

## End with the unresolved question

Sometimes agreement is unnecessary. You can establish which claims are supported, identify a genuine trade-off and leave the remaining judgment visible.

A useful closing sentence is: “We agree on these records, disagree about this consequence, and would need this evidence to test it.” That gives the conversation somewhere to go besides repeating labels.

## Try this

Split one policy dispute into three fields: factual claims, predictions and value judgments. Identify the evidence that could resolve one factual disagreement.
