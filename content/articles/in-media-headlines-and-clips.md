A clip can be authentic and still support a misleading claim. The recording, its caption and the interpretation attached to it are separate pieces of evidence.

Mike Caulfield’s SIFT method includes tracing claims, quotations and media to their original context. That is more useful than deciding whether a post feels convincing because it has a familiar logo or many shares. [1](https://hapgood.us/2019/06/19/sift-the-four-moves/)

## Write down the precise claim

Is the post claiming that someone spoke these words, that they were spoken yesterday, that an event happened in a particular city, or that the words prove a particular policy position?

Each part may require a different check. A clear recording of a sentence does not establish its date. A correct date does not establish that a short excerpt fairly represents the full answer.

**Hypothetical example:** A speaker says, “Some people claim X; the evidence does not support it.” A cut containing only “X” could reverse the meaning. This example illustrates the risk; it is not an accusation about a real recording.

## Recover the surrounding material

Find the full speech, interview, proceeding or original upload where possible. Check the preceding question and the sentences immediately before and after the excerpt. Distinguish the event date from the upload date and the date a screenshot was circulated.

For translated material, compare the original audio or transcript with a reliable translation. If you cannot assess the language, say which translation you relied on rather than presenting your interpretation as independently established.

Reverse searching a distinctive frame or phrase can help locate earlier appearances. Treat a match as a lead: verify that it is the same material and inspect the context rather than relying on the search result’s caption.

## A headline can overstate a perfectly readable source

Ask whether the headline changes an estimate into a fact, a proposal into a decision, an allegation into a finding or a percentage-point change into a percentage change.

A real example comes from the Education Ministry’s UDISE+ 2025–26 release. Its broad narrative describes dropout declining across all levels, but the table reports a middle-stage rise from 3.5% to 3.6% between the final two years. The table supports a more specific account than the broad sentence. [2](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2282141&lang=1&reg=48)

The lesson is not that every sentence from that source is false. It is that even an original official release needs claim-by-claim reading.

## Do not turn uncertainty into a new accusation

If the original cannot be found, the claim remains unverified; that alone does not prove the clip was AI-generated or maliciously edited. Conversely, finding an original recording does not automatically validate every caption attached to it.

Save the original link and note the exact point being checked. Where a post exposes a private person or could provoke harassment, avoid amplifying it merely to ask whether it is true.

A useful correction identifies the exact error: “This clip is from this dated event; the circulating caption places it elsewhere.” It should link the evidence and avoid adding a speculative motive.
